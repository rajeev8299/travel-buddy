Varanasi is best known as a Hindu sacred city, but it is also deeply important in Buddhism and Jainism. For Buddhists, the nearby site of Sarnath is one of the most significant places in the world. For Jains, Varanasi is associated with several of the *tirthankaras*, the great teachers of their tradition.

This page separates **documented history** and **archaeological evidence** from **tradition** — the beliefs held by followers of each faith.

## Sarnath and the Buddha's first teaching

### Tradition & belief

**Buddhist tradition** holds that after attaining enlightenment at Bodh Gaya, the Buddha travelled to the Deer Park at Sarnath, near Varanasi. There he met five former companions and gave his first teaching, setting in motion the "wheel of the Dharma" (*Dharmachakra*). The five became the first members of the monastic community, the *Sangha*.

For this reason, Sarnath is counted among the four principal places of Buddhist pilgrimage, alongside Lumbini, Bodh Gaya and Kushinagar. [VERIFY: framing of the four principal sites — published source]

### Archaeological evidence

The archaeology of Sarnath shows that it became a major Buddhist centre:

- The Mauryan emperor **Ashoka** (3rd century BCE) built monuments here, including a polished stone pillar topped by the **Lion Capital**. The capital was found in excavations in 1904–05 and is now in the [Sarnath Museum](/places/heritage/sarnath-museum). India adopted it as its State Emblem in 1950.
- The [Dhamek Stupa](/places/heritage/dhamek-stupa) in its present form is largely a Gupta-period structure, generally dated to around the 5th–6th centuries CE, built over earlier foundations.
- The remains of monasteries spread across the site show that a large monastic community lived here over many centuries.

### Documented history

In the 7th century CE, the Chinese pilgrim **Xuanzang** visited Sarnath and described its monasteries and monks. [VERIFY: details of his account — published translation] The site later declined and was largely abandoned. From the 19th century onwards, surveys and excavations uncovered its monuments.

In the 20th century, Sarnath became a living pilgrimage centre again. The **Maha Bodhi Society** built the [Mulagandha Kuti Vihara](/places/heritage/mulagandha-kuti-vihara), which opened in 1931, and Buddhist communities from many countries have built temples and monasteries nearby.

### Visiting Sarnath

Sarnath makes an easy half-day trip from Varanasi. Start at the [Sarnath archaeological complex](/places/heritage/sarnath) and the Dhamek Stupa, then visit the museum and the modern temples. Walk around stupas clockwise, keep your voice low near monks at prayer, and ask before taking photographs of people. ## Varanasi in Jainism

### Tradition & belief

In Jainism, the *tirthankaras* are the spiritual teachers who showed the path to liberation. **Jain tradition** associates several of them with Varanasi and its surroundings:

- **Parshvanatha**, the 23rd tirthankara, is believed to have been born in Varanasi.
- **Suparshvanatha**, the 7th tirthankara, is also believed to have been born in Varanasi.
- **Shreyansanatha**, the 11th tirthankara, is associated with Sarnath, where a Jain temple stands near the Dhamek Stupa.
- **Chandraprabha**, the 8th tirthankara, is associated with Chandrapuri, near Varanasi.

### Visiting Jain temples

Jain temples have their own customs. Visitors are usually asked to remove shoes and leather items, to dress modestly and to avoid bringing food inside. Follow the instructions posted at the entrance and ask the temple staff if you are unsure.

## A shared landscape

Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions in Varanasi have influenced one another for more than two thousand years. The same ideas — renunciation, liberation, the sacredness of teaching — appear in different forms across all three. Standing at Sarnath, a few kilometres from the ghats, you can feel how closely these traditions sit together in this landscape.
