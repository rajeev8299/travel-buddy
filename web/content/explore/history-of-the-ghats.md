The **ghats** — broad flights of stone steps leading down to the Ganga — are Varanasi's most famous sight. They stretch along the western bank of the river from [Assi Ghat](/places/ghats/assi) in the south to [Raj Ghat](/places/ghats/raj) in the north. This page explains how they came to be built, what they are used for, and how to visit them well.

## How many ghats are there?

The number commonly given is **84**, though counts vary between sources depending on how adjoining ghats are grouped. [VERIFY: current official count — Varanasi Nagar Nigam] Each ghat has its own name, history and character, and you can walk the whole central stretch along the river when the water is low.

## Documented history: who built the ghats

Although worship at the river is very old, most of the stone ghats you see today were built relatively recently.

- **Around 1600**, Raja Man Singh of Amber built a palace above what is now Man Mandir Ghat. In the 1730s, Sawai Jai Singh II added an observatory on its roof — see [Jantar Mantar at Man Mahal](/places/heritage/jantar-mantar).
- **In the 18th century**, patrons from across India, especially Maratha rulers and their families, built or rebuilt many ghats along with temples and residences above them. **Ahilyabai Holkar** of Indore, under whom the present [Kashi Vishwanath Temple](/places/temples/kashi-vishwanath) was built in 1780, is remembered at [Ahilyabai Ghat](/places/ghats/ahilyabai). The Scindia family is remembered at [Scindia Ghat](/places/ghats/scindia).
- **In the 19th century**, building and rebuilding continued. British surveyors and artists recorded the riverfront in drawings and maps, which are now an important historical source. [VERIFY: key 19th-century records, e.g. James Prinsep's drawings of Benares — published source]
- **In recent years**, the riverfront has seen new development, including the redeveloped [Namo Ghat](/places/ghats/namo) in the north.

Many ghats are named after the patrons who built them, after the deities of the temples above them, or after events in sacred tradition.

## Tradition & belief

Many ghats carry stories from sacred tradition. A few examples:

- **[Dashashwamedh](/places/ghats/dashashwamedh)** — **tradition** holds that the god Brahma performed ten horse sacrifices (*dasha ashvamedha*) here.
- **[Manikarnika](/places/ghats/manikarnika)** — named, according to **tradition**, after a jewelled earring that fell here.
- **[Harishchandra](/places/ghats/harishchandra)** — linked in **tradition** to King Harishchandra, the legendary king of truth.
- **[Panchganga](/places/ghats/panchganga)** — believed by devotees to be the meeting place of five sacred rivers.
- **[Kedar](/places/ghats/kedar)** — a visit is believed to carry the merit of a pilgrimage to Kedarnath.

These stories are central to how pilgrims experience the riverfront, though they belong to tradition rather than historical record.

## What the ghats are used for

The ghats are not monuments but working spaces, used from before dawn until late at night:

- **Bathing and prayer** — the main purpose of most ghats.
- **Ceremonies** — the evening Ganga Aarti at Dashashwamedh and other ghats; festivals such as [Dev Deepawali](/festivals/dev-deepawali) and [Nag Nathaiya](/festivals/nag-nathaiya) at [Tulsi Ghat](/places/ghats/tulsi).
- **Cremation** — only at Manikarnika and Harishchandra.
- **Daily life** — washing clothes, teaching, yoga, wrestling, music, cricket, chai stalls and conversation.
- **Boats** — boat families moor along the steps and take visitors out on the river.

## The ghats through the year

When the river is low (roughly October to June), you can walk along the base of the steps from one ghat to the next. During the **monsoon** (roughly July to September), the river can rise to cover the lower steps and even reach the buildings above, and walking along the riverfront may not be possible. ## Visiting the ghats

- **Best times:** early morning, for the light and the bathing rituals; and dusk, for the Aarti. A [morning walk along the ghats](/things-to-do/morning-ghat-walk) or a [boat ride](/things-to-do/sunrise-boat-ride) is the best introduction.
- **Footing:** the steps are uneven and can be steep, wet or slippery. Wear shoes with good grip and take your time. Older visitors may prefer to see the ghats from a boat, or to visit the wider, more level ghats such as [Assi](/places/ghats/assi).
- **Respect:** dress modestly, ask before photographing people, and keep a respectful distance from rituals. At the cremation ghats, never photograph cremations or mourners.
- **Touts:** you may be approached with offers of boats, guides or "explanations". Decline politely if you're not interested, and arrange guides in advance where possible.
