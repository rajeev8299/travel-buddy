Varanasi is often described as one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world. That claim is widely repeated and difficult to prove exactly, but the city's history is undoubtedly very long. This page separates what is **documented** in historical records, what **archaeology** shows, and what **tradition** holds, so you can see how the layers fit together.

## Archaeological evidence: the earliest city

The earliest physical evidence for settlement comes from the **Rajghat plateau**, at the northern end of the modern riverfront near [Raj Ghat](/places/ghats/raj). Excavations there have uncovered layers of occupation going back to a very early period. This means the original city stood further north than the busy central ghats of today. Over the centuries the centre of gravity shifted south along the river.

## Documented history: the kingdom of Kashi

By around the 6th century BCE, **Kashi** was one of the sixteen great kingdoms (*mahajanapadas*) of north India named in early Buddhist texts. It was famous for its trade and its textiles — a reputation the city still has today. [VERIFY: dating and the textual references — published histories]

**Sarnath**, a short distance north-east of the city, became central to Buddhism. Buddhist tradition places the Buddha's first teaching there, and **archaeological evidence** shows that the Mauryan emperor Ashoka built monuments at Sarnath in the 3rd century BCE, including a pillar topped by the famous Lion Capital. See [Sarnath](/places/heritage/sarnath).

In the 7th century CE, the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang visited the region and described its monasteries and temples. [VERIFY: details of Xuanzang's account — published translation]

## Medieval Varanasi

In the 11th and 12th centuries the city was an important centre under the **Gahadavala** dynasty. [VERIFY: Gahadavala capital and dates — published histories]

Over the following centuries, Varanasi experienced repeated destruction of temples during periods of conflict, followed by rebuilding. Historical sources record attacks in the late 12th century, and later episodes of destruction and reconstruction under different rulers. [VERIFY: specific events and dates — published histories] This cycle is one reason why few of the city's temples today are older than the 18th century, even though worship at many sites is far older.

## The Mughal period

Under the Mughal emperor Akbar in the 16th century, Hindu nobles at his court sponsored new building in Varanasi. Raja Man Singh of Amber built the palace now known as **Man Mahal** above Man Mandir Ghat around 1600; in the 1730s, Sawai Jai Singh II added an astronomical observatory on its roof. See [Jantar Mantar at Man Mahal](/places/heritage/jantar-mantar).

In 1669, during the reign of Aurangzeb, the Vishwanath temple of that time was destroyed. The [Alamgir Mosque](/places/heritage/alamgir-mosque) above Panchganga Ghat also dates from his reign. Questions about the history of some sites — including the Gyanvapi complex beside the present Kashi Vishwanath Temple — are the subject of ongoing legal proceedings, and we do not take a position on them.

## The 18th century: Maratha patrons and the Benares state

Much of the Varanasi you see today took shape in the **18th century**. Patrons from across India, especially Maratha rulers and their families, built and rebuilt ghats, temples and palaces along the river. The most famous example is **Ahilyabai Holkar**, queen of Indore, under whom the present [Kashi Vishwanath Temple](/places/temples/kashi-vishwanath) was built in **1780**.

In the same century a local dynasty, the rulers of Benares (the **Kashi Naresh**), established their seat across the river at [Ramnagar Fort](/places/heritage/ramnagar-fort), built in the 18th century by Raja Balwant Singh. In **1781**, Raja Chet Singh clashed with East India Company forces under Warren Hastings — an episode remembered at [Chet Singh Ghat](/places/ghats/chet-singh) — and the city came increasingly under British influence.

## Colonial and modern Varanasi

During the 19th century British officials surveyed and documented the city; James Prinsep's drawings and maps of Benares are an important record from this period. [VERIFY: date and title of Prinsep's published work]

In **1916**, Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya founded [Banaras Hindu University](/explore/banaras-hindu-university), which became one of India's major universities. After independence, the princely state of Benares joined the Indian Union, and the city's official name became Varanasi. [VERIFY: dates of accession and of the official name change — Government of India / Uttar Pradesh records]

In recent years, large infrastructure projects have changed parts of the city. The **Kashi Vishwanath Dham corridor**, inaugurated in **December 2021**, opened up the area around the temple and created a direct route down to the river. See [Modern Varanasi and tourism](/explore/modern-varanasi).

## Tradition & belief

Alongside documented history, Varanasi has a rich body of sacred tradition. **Tradition** holds that Kashi is Shiva's own city, that it was founded at the beginning of time, and that it rests on the tip of Shiva's trident, beyond the reach of destruction. These beliefs are central to how many visitors and residents understand the city, and you'll hear them often — but they belong to faith and tradition rather than to historical record. Read more in [Religious and spiritual significance](/explore/spiritual-significance).
