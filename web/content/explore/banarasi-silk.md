Banarasi silk is one of India's most celebrated textiles. A fine Banarasi saree, with its patterns woven in gold and silver thread, is a treasured possession worn at weddings and festivals across the country. This page explains how it is made, who makes it, and how to buy it fairly.

## A long tradition

## How Banarasi silk is woven

A traditional Banarasi saree is woven on a **handloom**. The process involves several specialists:

1. **Design.** A pattern is drawn and then translated into a set of punched cards or a programmed mechanism that tells the loom which threads to lift. Many traditional handlooms use a **jacquard** attachment for this.
2. **Preparation.** Silk threads are dyed, wound and set up on the loom — a slow, skilled job.
3. **Weaving.** The weaver passes the weft threads back and forth by hand. The decorative patterns are woven using **zari**, a thread wrapped in metal (traditionally silver, sometimes gilded with gold).
4. **Finishing.** The finished fabric is checked, trimmed and folded.

A complex saree can take weeks or even months to complete. [VERIFY: typical weaving times — artisan organisations]

### Common styles and terms

- **Kinkhab** — heavy brocade with dense gold or silver patterning.
- **Jangla** — patterns of sprawling vines and flowers.
- **Butidar** — fabric scattered with small motifs (*buti*).
- **Tanchoi** — a style with intricate, often small-scale patterns woven in silk.
- **Jamdani-style** — fine, lightweight fabrics with woven motifs.

## Who weaves Banarasi silk

The craft is carried on by families of weavers, many of whom have worked at it for generations. A large part of the weaving community in Varanasi is Muslim, concentrated in neighbourhoods such as **[Madanpura](/markets/madanpura)** and other areas of the city, while traders and designers come from many communities. The craft is a striking example of how Varanasi's different communities work together.

You can still hear the clack of handlooms in the lanes of the weavers' quarters. A [silk weaving visit](/things-to-do/silk-weaving-visits) with a local guide is the best way to see it.

## Handloom, powerloom and imitation

Today, much "Banarasi-style" fabric is made on **powerlooms** or outside the region, and some is made with synthetic threads. These products are cheaper, which can make it hard for handloom weavers to earn a living.

Ways to recognise handloom:

- **Look at the back.** On a handloom saree, the back of the patterned area often shows loose threads (floats) where the zari passes from one motif to the next. Powerloom fabric may look neater at the back.
- **Look for small irregularities.** Hand-weaving produces tiny variations that machines don't.
- **Ask about the GI tag and handloom marks**, and about the type of zari used. [VERIFY: current certification marks for handloom and GI products — Ministry of Textiles / GI Registry]
- **Price.** Genuine handloom silk with real zari is expensive. A very low price for a "pure silk handloom" saree is a warning sign.

## Buying fairly

- **Take your time.** Don't let anyone hurry you into a purchase, and don't feel obliged to buy after a visit.
- **Buy direct where you can.** Buying from weavers or their cooperatives means more of the price reaches the people who made the cloth.
- **Be wary of commission tours.** Some guides and drivers take visitors to shops that pay them commission, which raises the price. Our partners take no shopping commission.
- **Ask questions.** Ask what the fabric is made of, whether it was handwoven, and what kind of zari was used. A good seller will explain.
- **Keep your receipt** and ask about returns before you pay.
